Overview

Every commodity boom starts with a genuine supply shock. In late 2022, demand for GPU capacity began expanding faster than any existing provider could meet it. Hyperscalers had neither the inventory nor the procurement agility to respond quickly, and the gap between what AI compute cost and what the market would pay was large enough to attract a new category of operator. Neoclouds — cloud providers built around GPU capacity — filled that gap. In Q2 2025 their quarterly revenues passed $5 billion, a 205% year-on-year increase.2 Analyst estimates for 2030 annual revenues range from $65 billion to $180 billion depending on category definition; the average across four forecasts is approximately $110 billion.3,4,5,6

The pricing arbitrage that created the category is straightforward. Hyperscalers price GPU capacity across a broad service portfolio and at margins calibrated to their full cost structure. Neoclouds price to fill GPU racks. An H100 DGX instance runs approximately $34 per hour from a neocloud versus $98 per hour from a major hyperscaler — the same hardware at a 65% discount.1 One third of all AI workloads now run outside the major cloud platforms as a result.7

What this document argues is that the category follows the same arc as other commodity industries: the supply shock attracts capital and innovation, both of which expand supply, which compresses the pricing gap that justified the investment. That does not mean neoclouds disappear. It means the business models that depend on scarcity will consolidate, and the ones that do not depend on it will remain. How the market is structured

A company in this market occupies some contiguous portion of a ten-column capability stack, running from physical infrastructure on the left to developer tooling on the right. Customers interact with the market at whatever abstraction level they need: a hyperscaler might lease raw power capacity; a startup building a voice agent might call a serverless inference API. The same GPU hardware sits underneath both. What differs is how many layers of software and service separate the customer from that hardware.

It is a cycle, not a bubble

The neocloud sector grew out of a supply shock. After late 2022, demand for GPU capacity expanded faster than hyperscalers or chipmakers could respond, and the resulting scarcity produced pricing that would be difficult to sustain in a competitive market. That gap attracted capital at scale — more than $35 billion in private neocloud funding in 2025 alone, tripling the prior year’s record36 — and the capital is now funding both additional infrastructure and the hardware innovation that will expand effective supply further. The pattern is a standard commodity response: high prices attract investment, investment increases supply, supply normalises prices.

This does not mean the sector collapses. It means it follows the same boom-bust cycle seen in commodities like oil, memory chips, and fibre-optic cable before it. Demand for AI compute is growing and will continue to grow. What is less certain is that demand growth matches supply growth 1-to-1.

Several mechanisms are expanding effective GPU capacity without requiring new chip production. NVIDIA’s Multi-Instance GPU technology allows a single physical chip to run up to seven isolated workloads in parallel — Red Hat benchmarks show a 2.3× throughput increase on the same hardware when properly configured for inference.A Cerebras’s wafer-scale CS-3 delivers 2.5 to 3 times the inference throughput of NVIDIA’s current-generation Blackwell DGX B200 on comparable large models,B and Groq’s Language Processing Unit runs 10 to 30 times the token output of an H100 at low batch sizes.C These are incremental gains applied to an existing installed base, which means effective AI compute supply is expanding significantly faster than the number of new GPUs shipped. The capital that flooded into GPU infrastructure in 2023 and 2024 is also still working its way through supply chains; infrastructure commitments made during the scarcity phase continue to come online regardless of whether demand keeps pace.

When effective supply catches demand, the wholesale GPU rental business comes under significant pressure. The business model depends on a spread between what a neocloud pays for compute capacity and what it charges customers. In an oversupplied market, both sides of that spread compress: wholesale costs fall, attracting more entrants at lower margins, which drives retail prices toward marginal cost. A bare-metal GPU rental business earning 14 to 16% gross margin after depreciation in normal conditions11 has very limited capacity to absorb that compression. 

The result is a convergence to a smaller number of vertically integrated companies (as we saw in the Web 1.0 and Web 2.0 consolidation phases for example). These are companies with the balance sheets to absorb companies struggling in a compute oversupply. We identify two archetypes whose valuations are more likely to survive a consolidation phase, because neither is primarily selling compute.

I. Sovereign clouds

Focus archetype I

In regulated sectors and government contexts across MENA and Africa, the question of where AI workloads run is determined by law rather than preference. Data localisation requirements, national security classification rules, and sector-specific compliance obligations all constrain which infrastructure an organisation can use, independent of price. A UAE government agency cannot route its workloads through a US-based data center because doing so is unlawful, not because CoreWeave’s offering is inadequate. The commercial comparison does not apply before the procurement conversation begins.

This regulatory environment has become more consequential as AI infrastructure has shifted from an IT procurement question to a policy one. Governments that previously had limited reason to care about where their data was processed now have direct political interest in whether AI models trained on national data operate inside national infrastructure. In the UAE and Saudi Arabia that interest has already translated into capital commitments at a scale that makes those countries among the largest non-US AI infrastructure investors in the world. Nigeria and Egypt are at an earlier stage — the policy conversation is ahead of the physical infrastructure — but the direction is consistent across the region. For commercial neoclouds, the consequence is a category of demand that is not addressable through better hardware, lower pricing, or more efficient operations.

The economics of sovereign clouds also differ from commercial neoclouds in ways that reduce exposure to commodity dynamics. Government-backed and sovereign wealth fund capital can absorb the long payback periods associated with greenfield infrastructure that commercial investors would not accept on a standard return timeline. Pricing is set against compliance requirements rather than against AWS spot rates. The risks specific to this archetype are also different in character: whether sovereign mandates generate sufficient commercial volume beyond government anchor customers to sustain operations at scale, and whether dependence on US chip exports undermines the practical meaning of the sovereignty being claimed. Both are political risks, not market ones, and both evolve on political rather than commercial timelines.

Africa

Africa is at an earlier point in the same trajectory. Nigeria had 17 operational data centers before 2026, none above 20 MW of capacity,30 and the infrastructure needed to host compliant AI workloads for financial services, healthcare, or government use cases is only now being built. The sovereign cloud thesis in Africa is partly an infrastructure build thesis: the regulatory environment exists or is developing, but the physical preconditions are only recently being established.

   

II. Software-depth platforms

Focus archetype II

The second archetype that survives a compute glut does so for a different reason: it is not selling compute in the first place. Software-depth platforms — which include serverless GPU infrastructure, fine-tuning services, latency-optimised inference, and developer tooling — sell abstraction. Customers pay for the platform’s software layer: the cold start performance, the serving optimisation, the fine-tuning pipelines, the observability tooling. The underlying GPU cost is an input, not the product. When that input deflates, the cost base improves while the switching costs that retain customers remain unchanged.

This matters because the switching cost in these businesses is accumulated tooling investment rather than hardware lock-in. A team that has built fine-tuning workflows, deployment automation, and monitoring on a given platform has invested engineering time that does not transfer to a competitor. That investment compounds with use: the longer a team operates on a platform, the more tooling it builds on top of it, and the higher the cost of migration. A cheaper GPU alternative, even a substantially cheaper one, does not automatically justify the disruption of rebuilding that infrastructure elsewhere. The same logic applies to serving optimisation: cold start performance, quantisation pipelines, and inference batching configurations tuned against real production traffic represent accumulated engineering depth that a new entrant with cheaper hardware cannot replicate immediately.

What this means during a compute glut is that these platforms are positioned to benefit from the same conditions that destroy the wholesale model. As GPU costs fall, the underlying input cost for these businesses falls too. Their customers do not leave because cheaper GPUs are available elsewhere — they stay because of the tooling and serving infrastructure built on top of those GPUs. The margin previously captured by the spread between wholesale and retail GPU pricing migrates toward the software layer. A developer team is not going to rebuild its production fine-tuning infrastructure because GPU rental rates dropped 40%, in the same way that a company does not move off Salesforce because a cheaper CRM exists.

Inference growth is also a tailwind for this archetype specifically. ABI Research projects inference will account for 80% of neocloud GPU demand by 2030.26 Inference workloads — particularly real-time voice, video, and agentic applications — are where serving optimisation creates the most visible performance differential. A well-tuned serving stack consistently delivers two to four times the throughput of a naive deployment on the same hardware, with cold start latency now below two seconds on leading platforms versus 30 to 60 seconds in 2024.21 As inference becomes the dominant workload, the value of that optimisation becomes more visible to more customers.

Economics

Software-depth platforms price on the value of the software layer, not on GPU rental rates. As underlying compute costs decline, margin on the software layer expands rather than compresses, because the switching cost that retains customers is independent of the GPU cost that constitutes the cost base. The business model resembles SaaS more than infrastructure rental: revenue is driven by platform adoption and depth of integration, and the cost base benefits from hardware commoditisation rather than being threatened by it because they don't own the underlying hardware. The risks are those typical of developer platforms — customer acquisition cost, competition from well-funded incumbents moving up the stack, and the possibility that the tooling ecosystem does not reach the depth required to generate durable switching costs before the market consolidates.

Conclusion

The neocloud market grew out of a specific set of conditions: GPU scarcity, insufficient hyperscaler capacity, and a surge in AI compute demand that outpaced existing infrastructure. Those conditions produced attractive economics for GPU rental businesses. They will not last indefinitely. The mechanisms that expand effective compute supply like hardware efficiency improvements, more efficient use of existing hardware, and the capital already committed to new data center capacity, increase the likelihood of a medium term oversupply. The pattern is typical in a commodity cycle, where demand and supply rarely move in tandem.

What that implies for the sector is not that neoclouds disappear, but that the businesses within it converge toward the positions with durable economics. Sovereign clouds are insulated from commodity dynamics because their customers’ choices are governed by law rather than cost comparison. Software-depth platforms are insulated because they are selling a software layer over commodity hardware, and the switching costs generated by deep tooling integration do not diminish as GPU prices fall. Both archetypes benefit from the same conditions that will compress the wholesale GPU rental model.
